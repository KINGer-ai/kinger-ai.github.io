XJTLU Museum describes the AI Dog Tour Project as a novel and highly interactive museum experience developed with the Academy of Artificial Intelligence and Advanced Technology [1]. The project is a suitable evaluation object because it combines museum communication with an AI-supported interaction. Visitors must understand how to begin, what the system can answer, where to go next, and what to do when the response is unclear.

The evaluation asks a practical question: can visitors complete the AI Dog tour tasks without confusion, unnecessary effort, or loss of confidence?
02 · Problem and motivation

Problem, motivation, and research questions

A museum AI system can be technically impressive but difficult to use. Visitors may not know which button starts the tour, whether the AI Dog understands spoken or typed questions, how to return to an exhibit, or what the system can and cannot do. These small interaction problems can stop the visit even when the underlying AI works correctly.

The AI Dog Tour is also a public-facing system. Visitors arrive with different levels of digital confidence and may use the system only once. The interaction therefore needs to be learnable without a manual. A task-based usability evaluation is a practical way to identify where users hesitate, make errors, or need help.

Research questions

RQ1 · Task success

Can visitors complete the core AI Dog tour tasks without critical failure?

RQ2 · Usability

How usable and efficient is the system according to task performance and SUS?

RQ3 · Barriers

What problems, risks, or improvement opportunities do participants report?

Hypotheses

  • H1: At least 80% of participants will complete each core task without critical failure.
  • H2: The median SUS score will meet the accepted usability benchmark.
  • H3: Participants will rate perceived ease more positively than their confidence in the AI Dog's answers.
  • H4: The main barriers will concern onboarding, navigation, or unclear system capability.

The study can support claims about the evaluated tasks, the tested system version, and the participant group. It cannot establish long-term adoption, learning outcomes, or the technical accuracy of every AI response.

03 · Background and related work

Background and related work

Usability is commonly defined through effectiveness, efficiency, and satisfaction [2]. For the AI Dog system, effectiveness means completing the tour tasks, efficiency means avoiding unnecessary steps and delays, and satisfaction includes ease of use and willingness to continue. A task-based test links these dimensions to observable behaviour.

The System Usability Scale provides a short global measure of perceived usability [3]. It is suitable here because participants can complete it in less than two minutes. Task success and observed assistance will provide behavioural evidence alongside the SUS score.

Human-AI interaction guidelines emphasise clear capability, efficient invocation, uncertainty cues, and easy correction [4]. These principles suggest that the evaluation should test not only whether visitors can start the tour, but also whether they can judge the answer and recover when the AI response is unclear.

Research gap: XJTLU Museum documents the AI Dog as an interactive project, but public evidence does not yet show whether visitors can operate it effectively during a museum tour.

04 · System and evaluation design

System and evaluation design

System and context

The AI Dog Tour Project supports an interactive museum visit. The study evaluates visitor-facing use of the system rather than its internal AI model. The tested interaction includes opening the tour, selecting or locating an exhibit, asking for information, following a route, and recovering from a response that the visitor does not understand.

The evaluation will use a current public or demonstration version of the system. If access is restricted, the team will request permission from the system owner and prepare a short recorded walkthrough or approved prototype for the session. The materials used by participants will remain the same across sessions so that usability findings are comparable.

A small task set keeps the session practical. It focuses on the actions every visitor must perform, rather than testing every feature. If a task cannot be completed independently, the moderator stops the attempt and records the point of failure. This prevents frustration while still providing useful evidence about the system.

Participants and sampling

The target sample is 12 to 15 XJTLU students or staff aged 18 or above. This meets the module guidance for a usability study and provides enough behavioural evidence to identify repeated interaction problems. Recruitment will use approved campus channels and a short sign-up form.

Participants do not need previous experience with the AI Dog, but the group will record prior museum and AI-tool use as descriptive context. The study does not compare separate groups or manipulate conditions. Every participant completes the same task sequence so that the results directly describe the usability of one system.

The four tasks are: (1) start the AI Dog tour and select an exhibit; (2) ask one exhibit-related question; (3) find and move to the next recommended stop; and (4) recover from an unclear or incorrect response. The moderator will use a standard script, provide no help unless the participant is unable to continue, and record assistance separately from independent success.

Ethics and safety

Data collection begins after ethics approval. Participants will receive an information sheet and consent form before the session. The study will not collect passwords, personal museum records, photographs of participants, or sensitive university information. If the AI Dog requires an account, the research team will use a prepared test account rather than asking participants to disclose personal credentials.

05 · Data preparation and analysis

Data preparation and analysis

SPSSDescriptive and SUS analysis
NotesThink-aloud theme summary
GitHubMaterials and report

Observation and questionnaire data will be entered into an Excel codebook with one row per participant. Task success will be coded as independent success, success with assistance, or failure. Time on task will be recorded in seconds. Errors and moderator interventions will be counted by task and category.

SPSS will be used for descriptive statistics. The report will include task success rates, medians or means for time on task, error frequencies, and an overall SUS score. The SUS result will be interpreted against published benchmark guidance [5]. Because the sample is small, the analysis will focus on practical patterns rather than complex inferential models.

Think-aloud comments and the short debrief will be summarised into a small number of usability themes, such as onboarding, navigation, answer clarity, and error recovery. Each theme will be supported by an anonymised example. The theme summary will be compared with the task data so that recommendations are based on observed problems rather than opinion alone.

The final analysis will report both positive and negative evidence. A task that works well should be described as a strength, not ignored. A repeated problem should include the number of participants affected and at least one concrete observation. This makes the recommendations clearer for Museum staff and easier to connect to future design changes.

07 · Discussion and recommendations

Discussion, limitations, and recommendations

The evaluation will show whether visitors can complete the AI Dog tour tasks without support and where the interaction creates difficulty. A high task success rate with a low SUS score would suggest that participants can finish the tasks but find the experience inefficient or frustrating. A low trust rating would suggest that participants do not understand the system's limits or reliability.

The discussion will connect each finding to a practical design action. For example, onboarding failures may require a clearer first screen, navigation errors may require better route feedback, and answer-clarity problems may require visible sources or confidence cues.

Limitations

  • The sample is small and may not represent all museum visitors.
  • The controlled task setting may differ from a normal exhibition visit.
  • The study evaluates one system version and selected tasks.
  • Moderator presence may influence participant behaviour.
  • No system-log access is assumed; observations are collected by the research team.

Recommendations

  1. Clarify how to start the tour and what the AI Dog can answer.
  2. Improve route feedback when participants move between exhibits.
  3. Provide a simple correction or retry path for unclear AI responses.
  4. Use short just-in-time instructions rather than a long manual.
  5. Run a second usability test after the design changes.
