Evaluating the XJTLU Museum AI guide

A two-group comparison of students' post-visit learning, attention, and interpretation during one museum visit.

Read abstract View poster

Thirty students rated nine post-visit outcomes. New knowledge showed the strongest descriptive difference, while engagement and interest were lower in the AI group. No comparison remained significant after Holm correction.

Abstract

A concise account of the question, two-group design, corrected result, and the boundary this sample can support.

Museum AI guides can offer flexible interpretation, but visitors judge a museum visit through more than factual delivery. Attention, understanding, personal meaning, emotional response, and connection to the exhibition all shape the experience. This report asks whether those post-visit ratings differed between students who used the XJTLU Museum AI guide and students who toured the same museum with exhibition labels only.

Thirty students took part, with 15 participants in each condition. Each participant rated nine outcomes on a five-point ordinal scale after one visit. Two-sided Mann-Whitney U tests compared the groups, and Holm correction controlled the family of nine exploratory comparisons. All 270 item ratings were valid.

No raw or corrected comparison reached .05. Q9 new knowledge showed the largest descriptive difference: 4.00 in the AI group and 3.53 in the labels-only group (difference = +0.47, raw p = .230, Holm p = 1.000). The AI group also rated emotional response and theme resonance higher, but rated engagement and interest lower. These differences are uncertain directions for further study. They do not confirm an effect. The sample does not establish equivalence, and the allocation record does not support a causal claim. The report therefore treats source grounding, object-level interpretation, route support, and recovery as design hypotheses to test with task-level evidence.

Open literature brief

Introduction

Museums are adopting conversational guides faster than they can establish what those guides add to a visit.

A museum guide is not a neutral delivery channel. Its voice, interaction pattern, and relationship to the exhibition decide what a visitor notices and how easily that visitor can connect an object to a wider story. Wang and Matviienko compared a generative AI chatbot with an existing museum app and found that guide form changed the visit [1]. Noh and Hong showed a related effect through reenacted characters: the guide's persona shaped engagement and emotional response as well as content access [2]. Both studies shift attention from whether AI is present to what the interaction allows a visitor to do.

Conversational access creates a second problem. Visitors need to know what to ask before the system can answer well. Tue, Pohl, and Merritt found that a conversational museum guide widened access to multimedia interpretation, but some visitors struggled to frame effective questions [3]. Xia et al. linked the quality of an AI guide's language to satisfaction, continued use, and recall [4]. The practical challenge is therefore twofold: the guide must provide useful interpretation, and the visitor must be able to steer the conversation without knowing the system in advance.

Those findings led to a comparison against the ordinary interpretive baseline already present in a museum, the exhibition label. The study measures post-visit experience as a set of related outcomes. This choice follows evidence that engagement, learning, meaning, and emotion do not always move together [8], and that engagement includes both absorption and sustained interest [9]. A guide may improve one part of the visit while weakening another.

Research question

Do post-visit experience ratings differ between students who used the XJTLU Museum AI guide and students who toured without it?

The questionnaire separates engagement, interest, new knowledge, understanding, reflection, personal meaning, context connection, emotional response, and theme resonance. This structure lets the analysis describe where the visit differed without collapsing the result into a single usability score.

Project overview

The report concentrates on the completed post-visit comparison and limits its claims to the evidence this dataset can carry.

The wider evaluation concerns the usability and visitor experience of the XJTLU Museum AI system. This report focuses on the completed questionnaire component because those data are complete, traceable, and suited to a transparent group comparison. It does not treat the survey as a full usability test.

Each participant contributed one post-visit observation. The report does not combine conditions within the same person, infer task success, or use the nine ratings as a substitute for observation. That boundary matters: a self-report comparison can show how students remembered the visit, but it cannot explain every moment that produced that judgement.

Background and related work

Fifteen sources establish three ideas behind the study: guide form changes experience, experience has several dimensions, and evaluation claims must match the evidence collected.

Guide form changes what visitors notice

Museum technology does not produce one uniform outcome called engagement. Zhang, Cao, and Hou compared a location-based museum game with a conventional visit and measured visitor experience separately from motivation, cognitive load, and objective learning [5]. The separation is important because a format can make a visit more active without making it easier or more educational.

Zhao et al. found a similar differentiation at the Shanghai Museum, where audio, smart-guide, and AR conditions produced different immersive and motivational profiles [6]. Their result supports a multi-item outcome set: a guide can improve one dimension while leaving another flat, and an average across all dimensions would hide that pattern.

Field evidence reveals design trade-offs

Field deployments expose choices that polished prototypes can hide. Boiano et al.'s voice-guide case shows how voice, pacing, and conversational framing meet real visitor behaviour [7]. That work has diagnostic value here: it identifies what to inspect during a visit. It does not estimate the size of the effect in this museum.

Experience needs more than one measure

Othman, Petrie, and Power developed the Museum Experience Scale around engagement, learning, meaning, and emotional connection [8]. O'Brien and Toms further separate absorption from sustained interest [9]. Together, these measures explain why a visitor can learn from a guide while finding the visit less absorbing, or remain interested while remembering very little content.

Falk and Dierking add context to that distinction. Visitors interpret an answer through personal interests, social expectations, and the physical exhibition [10]. The same sentence can therefore support meaning for one student and distract another. The present study cannot identify those situated processes, but it can show whether the outcome ratings moved in different directions.

Evaluation quality remains the bottleneck

Štekerová found that museum chatbot projects often describe development without measuring visitor experience [11]. Kamińska found a similar gap across generative AI deployments, where system descriptions are more common than evidence about visitor outcomes [12]. Those reviews make a null item-level result useful. The result cannot validate the guide, but it can stop the team from presenting a promising prototype as an established improvement.

Research gap

Museum studies show that guide design can change experience, yet item-level comparisons against a labels-only visit remain uncommon. This study adds a corrected local comparison and uses its descriptive pattern to locate design priorities. It answers a narrower question than a full usability programme: whether students who used this guide rated selected post-visit outcomes differently from students who did not.

Methods

A between-participant visit comparison, one short questionnaire, and an exploratory rank-based analysis.

Procedure

  1. Information and consent

    Participants read the study information and agreed to take part before joining a condition.

  2. Visit condition

    One group used the XJTLU Museum AI guide. The comparison group toured the same exhibition using labels only.

  3. Post-visit survey

    Every participant completed the same nine experience ratings on a five-point disagree-agree scale.

  4. Check and analyse

    The team checked consent, recent-visit status, group coding, item ranges, and missing values before analysis.

The data describe one visit per participant. The report does not treat repeated ratings from the same person as independent observations.

Mann-Whitney U test

Mann and Whitney's rank test fits ordinal items and two independent groups [13]. The test asks whether one condition's ratings tend to rank higher than the other. Group means appear in the results because they are familiar, but they do not carry the inference by themselves.

Holm correction

Holm's procedure controls the family-wise error rate across the nine comparisons [14]. The smallest raw p-value is tested first, then each remaining value against a progressively stricter threshold. Corrected values of 1.000 therefore mean that no item remained distinguishable after the family of tests was considered.

Signed Z and effect direction

Signed Z values describe rank direction. Positive values favour the no-AI group; negative values favour the AI group. Kerby's rank-biserial formula guides interpretation of that direction [15]. Direction helps locate a design hypothesis, but it is not evidence that the condition caused the difference.

Results

The group means moved in both directions. No item remained statistically distinguishable after correction. The result leaves a descriptive pattern and does not confirm an effect.

A null result with a usable descriptive pattern

Raw p-values ranged from .230 to .875, and every Holm-adjusted value was 1.000. The sample therefore does not support a claim that the guide changed any item. It also cannot establish equivalence: a small or uneven effect could remain hidden in 15 participants per group.

Discussion and recommendations

The comparison cannot justify a general claim that AI improved the visit. It can still show where interpretation helped, where the visit experience weakened, and what the next evaluation must measure.

What the comparison can support

Five outcomes had higher AI-group means. Q9 new knowledge produced the largest difference at +0.47, followed by emotional response at +0.40 and theme resonance at +0.33. These values describe this sample. They do not establish that the guide produced the differences, especially because the allocation record is not clean enough to support random assignment.

The pattern is compatible with earlier work in which conversational access and language framing shaped learning-related responses [1], [3], [4]. It is also compatible with the multi-dimensional view of museum experience [8], [9]. The guide may provide an extra interpretive layer, but the present data cannot separate that layer from novelty, prior interest, or differences between participants.

Why learning and engagement may diverge

Engagement and interest were lower in the AI group by 0.33 and 0.27 mean points. The difference is statistically uncertain, but its direction is consistent with a plausible design trade-off: an additional question-answer layer can supply facts while interrupting the visual and spatial rhythm of a visit.

  • The guide may ask visitors to formulate a query before they know what the exhibition offers.
  • A long answer can compete with looking at the object and reduce visual attention.
  • A failed question can end the interaction when no recovery route is visible.

Falk and Dierking's account of personal, social, and physical context explains why these costs vary between visitors [10]. The right design response is not to add more content. It is to make each answer shorter, more closely tied to the object, and easier to continue.

What the next evaluation should measure

The next study should record what the visitor does before, during, and after the guide response. Useful measures include task completion, time to a useful answer, whether the source is visible, whether the visitor can continue without moderator help, and whether a failed question can be repaired.

A larger sample should also pre-specify one primary outcome and report the other items as secondary. The current nine exploratory tests locate signals. They cannot confirm an item-level effect. Reviews of museum chatbot evaluation reach the same conclusion: system description must be paired with evidence about visitor experience [11], [12].

Recommendations

Specific changes to the museum AI guide

Use approved museum content, explain one object at a time, support a short visitor route, and provide recovery controls.

Apply the four changes as one visitor flow. Grounded records make each answer traceable. Object-level explanation keeps the answer specific. Route cues turn each answer into a next step. Recovery controls keep the visitor moving after an unclear response. Each feature should be useful on its own and should also improve the transition to the next part of the visit.

Test the prototype with source accuracy, source visibility, task success, recovery rate, time to a useful answer, and the nine post-visit ratings. Record which recommendation produced each change. This keeps the evidence attributable to a specific interface decision.

Ground answers in approved museum records

Retrieve the current exhibit label, object record, or curatorial note before generating an answer. Display the source name and last-updated date beside the response. When approved content is unavailable, label the point unverified and direct the visitor to the nearest relevant exhibit. Keep the retrieval order visible so visitors can tell whether a claim came from the museum, from general model knowledge, or from no verified record.

Keep every explanation tied to one exhibit object

Open with the exhibit name, add one factual detail, connect it to one broader theme, and close with one question about what the visitor noticed. Keep the first answer to two or three sentences. Place further detail behind a separate action. This structure gives the visitor a visible object, a reason to look, and a way to answer without turning the interaction into a lecture.

Use each answer to support the next visit step

Recommend one next object or one comparison after each answer. Show the visitor's position within a three-to-five-stop route. Let the visitor choose when to continue; do not advance the route automatically. The next suggestion should explain the relation to the current object. Keep the visitor oriented before directing attention to another exhibit.

Build recovery into the conversation and record it

Provide rephrase, “not what I meant”, next-exhibit, and map controls. After an unclear answer, ask which part was wrong and keep the last valid object in context. Log the query intent, failed response, recovery action, and outcome. Recovery belongs to the visitor experience. Treat it as a measured part of the interaction.

Prototype test: ask participants to find one sourced answer, continue to a related exhibit, and recover from an ambiguous question introduced for the test. Compare completion and time against the labels-only baseline before repeating the nine post-visit ratings.
