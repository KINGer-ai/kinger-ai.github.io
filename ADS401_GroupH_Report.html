Evaluating the XJTLU Museum AI guide

A 30-student comparison of post-visit experience with an AI guide and a labels-only museum visit.

Read abstract View poster

Thirty students formed two equal groups and rated nine post-visit outcomes. No item reached significance after Holm correction.

01

Abstract

This report compares post-visit experience after an AI-guided museum visit with a labels-only visit.

Objective. The study asks whether a museum AI guide changes how visitors rate a visit. Experience was measured as nine separate outcome ratings.

Method. Thirty XJTLU students took part. Fifteen used the XJTLU Museum AI guide and fifteen followed the same exhibition with labels only. Participants rated nine outcomes from 1 to 5. The groups were compared with Mann-Whitney U tests and Holm correction.

Results. No comparison survived correction. The largest descriptive gap appeared for Q9 new knowledge: 4.00 in the AI group and 3.53 in the no-AI group, with raw p = .230 and Holm p = 1.000. Signed Z values ranged from -1.199 to +1.084.

Conclusion. This sample did not show a reliable item-level difference. It does not show that the two visits were equivalent, nor that the AI guide caused no effect. A larger study with a verified allocation procedure and a pre-specified primary outcome is needed.

02

Introduction

The project began with a simple problem: museum AI guides are being introduced faster than their effects on visitors are being tested.

The closest precedent is Wang and Matviienko's field experiment, which placed a generative museum chatbot against an existing museum app [1]. The comparison shows that guide form changes the visit. Noh and Hong reach a related point through reenacted characters, whose persona shaped engagement and emotional response [2]. Together, the studies ask what the visitor can do with the guide.

Tue et al. bring that design question into exhibition use: a conversational guide widened access to multimedia content, but some visitors struggled to frame useful questions [3]. Xia et al. connect the difficulty to language, showing that an AI guide's wording shaped satisfaction, continued use, and recall [4]. Read together, these findings suggest that interpretation can improve even when interaction remains fragile. That tension led to the present comparison with a labels-only visit across nine post-visit outcomes.

Research question

Do post-visit experience ratings differ between students who used the XJTLU Museum AI guide and students who toured without it?

The nine outcomes are engagement, interest, new knowledge, understanding, reflection, meaning, context, emotion, and theme resonance.

04

Background and related work

The review draws on 15 sources. It starts with comparisons between guide formats, then narrows to measurement and evaluation quality.

01

Guide modality changes experience

Guide format changes the mix of outcomes a visitor reports. Zhang, Cao, and Hou separated experience from motivation, cognitive load, and objective learning in a location-based game [5].

Zhao et al. found a similar differentiation at the Shanghai Museum, where audio, smart-guide, and AR conditions produced different immersive and motivational profiles [6]. This supports a multi-item outcome set.

02

Deployment evidence needs boundaries

Field deployments expose choices that prototypes can hide. Boiano et al.'s voice-guide case shows how those choices meet real visitor behaviour [7]. Its value here is diagnostic: it identifies what to inspect, while leaving the size of any effect for controlled comparison.

03

Visitor experience has several dimensions

Othman, Petrie, and Power developed the Museum Experience Scale around engagement, learning, meaning, and emotional connection [8]. O'Brien and Toms further separate absorption from sustained interest [9].

That distinction matters for our result. A guide can support learning while leaving attention flat or making the visit feel less absorbing.

04

Context and evidence quality

Falk and Dierking place interpretation within personal, social, and physical context, so the same answer can mean different things to different visitors [10].

The evaluation literature adds a warning. Štekerová found that museum chatbot projects often describe development without measuring visitor experience [11], and Kamińska found the same gap across generative-AI deployments [12]. These reviews make a null result useful: it can identify where design should improve, even when it cannot establish an average effect.

Research gap

Museum studies show that guide design can change experience, yet corrected comparisons against a labels-only visit remain uncommon. This experiment supplies that local test and uses its null result to locate design priorities; it does not claim equivalence.

Mann-Whitney U test

Mann and Whitney's rank test fits the ordinal items and independent groups [13]. Means remain descriptive.

Holm correction

Holm's procedure controls the family-wise error rate across the nine comparisons [14]. The corrected threshold is evaluated in rank order.

Signed Z and effect direction

Signed Z values describe rank direction. Positive values favour the no-AI group; negative values favour the AI group. Kerby's rank-biserial formula guides interpretation of that direction [15].

07

Discussion and recommendations

The means do not justify a general claim that AI improved the visit. They do identify where the next prototype should concentrate.

What deserves credit

Five outcomes had higher AI-group means. This does not establish a benefit, but it makes the guide's role as an extra interpretive layer plausible. Q9 new knowledge carried the clearest signal at +0.47.

The pattern fits earlier findings that conversational access and language framing can shape learning-related responses. Explanation and access carry that promise. A visible AI feature adds little by itself.

What remains weak

  • No adjusted comparison reached .05, so no item-level advantage is established.
  • Engagement and interest were descriptively lower, which weakens the case for the current visit loop.
  • The allocation record, single-site sample, and self-report design prevent a causal claim.
  • Task success, timing, question quality, and recovery were not measured.

Why improvement matters

Visitors need an answer they can use: a detail they notice, a link to what they know, and a way back when a question fails.

Our strongest promise and clearest gap appeared on different outcomes: knowledge interpretation rose, while engagement did not. The next design cycle should close that gap before the guide is scaled.

Recommendations

Specific changes to the museum AI guide

Keep AI as a traceable interpretation layer. Rebuild the visit around questions, sources, and recovery.

The earlier studies place question formulation and language at the centre of guide experience. Our means add a local constraint: knowledge interpretation is the clearest promise, while engagement and interest need more work.

Each change names its evidence. Treat them as hypotheses for the next prototype and task-based test; none depends on claiming a statistically significant effect.

Q9 learning | AI 4.00 vs 3.53

Show the source behind each answer

Keep exhibit-specific facts, then show the matching label, object, or collection source beside every answer. Limit the reply to two or three sentences and end with one follow-up question. This protects the learning promise while making generated content easier to trust.

Q7 engagement | AI 3.47 vs 3.80

Guide visitors through a short route

Offer a three-to-five-stop route, prompt visitors to look closely or compare two objects, and show progress between stops. Add one reason to continue after each answer. The guide needs a visit rhythm beyond its search field.

Q8 interest, Q10 understanding, Q12 meaning

Connect each answer to object and visitor

Repeat the relevant label, connect one factual detail to a broader story, and ask what the visitor already noticed. Keep that bridge short. A long response can pull attention away from the object.

No task-level evidence yet

Make misunderstanding easy to repair

Add rephrase, “not what I meant”, next exhibit, and location controls. Log failed questions and the path back to a useful answer. The next study should verify allocation, pre-specify one primary outcome, and pair task success with the questionnaire.
