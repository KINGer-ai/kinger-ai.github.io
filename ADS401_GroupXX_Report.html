Participants XJTLU students

Design Sequential mixed methods

Survey evidence followed by observed task performance and semi-structured interviews.

XJTLU presents XIPU AI Agent as part of its “Education + AI” strategy. The platform supports knowledge-base construction, plugin integration, workflow automation, and visual evaluation [1]. However, platform availability does not show whether students can discover the system, choose an appropriate agent, complete academic tasks, or calibrate their trust in its outputs.

The evaluation question is not whether XIPU AI Agent is universally “good.” It is when, for whom, and under what conditions the agent supports student academic work.

This project therefore evaluates one bounded context: student use of XIPU AI Agent for academic-support tasks. The report distinguishes prior evidence, the planned study, observed participant data, interpretation, and limitations. No participant data have been invented for this draft.

02 · Problem and motivation

Problem, motivation, and research questions

Universities are integrating conversational AI into learning services at a pace that often exceeds evidence about actual student use. XIPU AI and XIPU AI Agent expand access to generative AI, but students may encounter several uncertainties: whether the service exists, which agent is suitable for a task, whether answers are reliable, whether use is permitted in a module, and whether the system protects their data.

These uncertainties matter for HCI because adoption is not produced by technical capability alone. Users must interpret system boundaries, form expectations, recover from errors, and decide how much to trust the output. A system can be available but poorly discovered; technically capable but hard to operate; or useful but distrusted because its limitations are unclear.

Research questions

RQ1 · Awareness and use

What do XJTLU students know about XIPU AI Agent, and how do they currently use it for academic support?

RQ2 · Usability

How effectively, efficiently, and satisfactorily can students complete representative academic-support tasks?

RQ3 · Trust and adoption

How are perceived usefulness, ease of use, trust, and continued-use intention associated?

RQ4 · Barriers

What barriers, risks, and improvement opportunities emerge from participants' observed and reported experiences?

Hypotheses

  • H1: Perceived usefulness and ease of use will be positively associated with continued-use intention.
  • H2: Trust will mediate the association between perceived usefulness and continued-use intention.
  • H3: At least 80% of usability participants will complete each core task without critical failure.
  • H4: Interview themes will identify uncertainty about answer reliability, academic-integrity boundaries, and task fit.

The study can support claims about XJTLU students, the selected tasks, and the evaluated version of XIPU AI Agent. It cannot establish institution-wide adoption, long-term learning gains, or the accuracy of every agent response.

03 · Background and related work

Background and related work

Technology acceptance research identifies perceived usefulness and ease of use as central predictors of intention to use a system [2]. UTAUT extends this account with social influence and facilitating conditions [3]. Those constructs remain relevant to AI agents because students need to understand what the system does and how to access support when tasks become difficult.

Trust adds a further condition. Conversational agents can produce fluent answers that vary in accuracy, making trust calibration a design problem rather than only a user attitude. The CHI guidelines for human-AI interaction emphasise making capabilities clear, supporting efficient invocation, explaining uncertainty, and enabling correction [4]. These principles suggest that evaluating an educational agent should combine perceived measures with observed task success and error recovery.

Usability evaluation contributes behavioural evidence. The System Usability Scale provides a compact global measure [5], while task success, completion time, and observed errors show whether users can act effectively. A mixed-methods design can then explain why favourable or unfavourable ratings occur.

Research gap: XJTLU has an official AI-agent platform with documented capabilities, but public evidence does not yet show how students discover and use it for academic-support tasks, where usability breaks down, or how trust shapes continued use.

The planned evaluation combines these literatures. Acceptance measures address intention and perceived value; trust measures address confidence and reliance; usability measures address observable performance; qualitative evidence explains contextual barriers. This triangulation is more informative than asking participants only whether they “like” the system.

04 · System and evaluation design

System and evaluation design

System and collaboration context

XIPU AI Agent is described by XJTLU as a platform for building and deploying AI agents across teaching, learning, research, and institutional management. Its reported capabilities include agent creation, knowledge bases, plugin integration, workflow automation, and performance evaluation [1]. This study evaluates the student-facing use of existing agents rather than the platform's administrative authoring tools.

Participants and sampling

Participants will be XJTLU students aged 18 or above. Recruitment will use approved university channels, course announcements where permission is granted, and snowball recruitment. The sample will include students with and without prior XIPU AI Agent experience so that awareness and non-use can be examined without assuming adoption.

The survey target is 100 complete responses. A minimum of approximately 84 participants is needed to detect a moderate correlation of r = .30 with 80% power at α = .05, two-tailed; the additional participants allow for missingness and exclusions. The usability phase will recruit 12 to 15 students, meeting the module guidance for a usability study and providing sufficient behavioural evidence for within-session analysis.

Ethics, privacy, and bias mitigation

Formal data collection will begin only after the required ethics process is approved. Consent will explain voluntary participation, withdrawal, data storage, anonymisation, and the limits of confidentiality. No passwords, assessment answers, identifiable records, or sensitive university information will be entered into XIPU AI Agent or included in published materials.

Recruitment will avoid coercive messaging and will not rely only on students who already favour AI. A recruitment log will record channel and eligibility. Interview extracts will be anonymised with participant codes, and small demographic combinations will be suppressed. The research team will document deviations from the protocol rather than silently replacing them.

05 · Data preparation and analysis

Data preparation and analysis

ExcelData entry and coding
SPSSDescriptive and inferential tests
NVivo / ExcelQualitative coding
GitHubWeb report and materials

Survey and usability data will be exported to Excel for cleaning, harmonisation, and codebook creation, then imported into SPSS for analysis. Each variable will have a defined type, scale, permissible range, and reverse-coding rule. Duplicate and bot-like survey responses will be screened against prespecified criteria rather than removed solely because their values are inconvenient.

Quantitative analysis will begin with missing-data inspection, distributions, outliers, and internal consistency. Cronbach's alpha and item-total correlations will be reported for multi-item scales. Descriptive statistics will summarise awareness, adoption, task performance, usability, trust, and intention. Pearson correlations will be used when assumptions are met; Spearman's rho or robust alternatives will be reported otherwise.

Multiple regression will test whether perceived usefulness, ease of use, and trust are associated with continued-use intention after relevant controls. A bootstrap mediation model will examine perceived trust as a pathway between usefulness and intention [6]. Assumptions will be inspected using residual plots, influence diagnostics, and collinearity statistics. Standardised effect sizes, exact p-values, and 95% confidence intervals will be reported.

Interview recordings will be transcribed de-identified and coded using reflexive thematic analysis. The team will create an initial codebook, meet to resolve coding differences, and organise codes into candidate themes. Negative and conflicting cases will be reported. Findings will be triangulated with task observations and survey patterns rather than treated as a substitute for quantitative evidence.

07 · Discussion and recommendations

Discussion, limitations, and recommendations

The expected contribution is an evidence chain linking a specific XJTLU AI-agent context to observable usability, trust, and adoption mechanisms. If students cannot discover the system or identify a suitable agent, then improvements in model quality will not translate into use. If task success is high but trust remains low, the problem may lie in transparency, boundaries, or support rather than raw capability.

Planned interpretation

The final discussion will connect each research question to evidence, distinguish statistically supported associations from descriptive patterns, and identify findings that conflict with the hypotheses. It will avoid causal language for the cross-sectional survey. Recommendations will identify the responsible actor, such as XIPU AI staff, module teams, or students, and state the evidence supporting each recommendation.

Known limitations

  • Convenience and snowball sampling may overrepresent digitally confident students.
  • Self-report responses may be affected by social desirability and limited introspection.
  • Usability sessions create a controlled setting and may show novelty effects.
  • No system logs are assumed; self-reported use will not be described as system evidence.
  • The study evaluates selected tasks and one university, limiting generalisation.
  • Cross-sectional associations cannot establish the direction of causal effects.

Planned recommendation areas

  1. Improve first-use discovery by explaining which tasks the platform supports and which it does not.
  2. Clarify academic-integrity boundaries and data-handling expectations at the point of use.
  3. Support trust calibration with source visibility, uncertainty cues, and clear error recovery.
  4. Reduce task breakdowns through agent selection guidance, examples, and reusable prompts.
  5. Evaluate future iterations with representative tasks and participants, including non-users.
